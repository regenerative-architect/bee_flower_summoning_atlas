Scope & Honesty, Up Front 📐

It's worth saying plainly, because it changes how you should use this page: bee taxonomy has exactly seven families. That number doesn't grow — it's not a data-collection gap, it's the actual shape of bee evolution, the same way there are only so many families of cats or oak trees. Anyone offering you "1,000 bee families" is offering you a fiction.

What does have real room to grow is the layer underneath: roughly 500–580 genera and about 20,000 species worldwide, most of them solitary, ground-nesting, and quietly unnoticed. That's where this atlas expands — see the Genus Directory below.

One more honesty note, on the word "unextinct": planting flowers cannot revive a species that is genuinely, globally extinct. That is permanent, and no habitat design changes it. What planting can do — prevent further extinctions, and help a species that survives elsewhere recolonize a place it disappeared from — is covered directly in Extinction & Memory, without overstating it.

Bloom Calendar: Keep the Nectar Gate Open 📅

This calendar is written for a temperate Northern Hemisphere year. If you're gardening in the Southern Hemisphere (much of the Stenotritidae, Colletidae, and Melittidae range covered here), shift every season by roughly six months — your "early spring" is around September, not March.

Early Spring

Willow, maple, serviceberry, cherry, spring ephemerals, blueberry relatives. Target: Andrenidae, Colletidae, early Apidae, mason bees.

Late Spring

Apple, raspberry, clover, lupine, penstemon, borage. Target: Megachilidae, Apidae, Andrenidae.

Summer

Bee balm, hyssop, sunflower, coneflower, milkweed, mint, tomato/blueberry buzz flowers, squash blooms at dawn. Target: Bombus, Megachile, Halictidae, Peponapis.

Fall

Asters, goldenrod, late sunflower, native grasses with nesting cover. Target: overwintering queens, late sweat bees, cellophane bees, honey bees.

Extinction & Memory 🕯️

This section exists because the instinct behind "bring them back" is a good one — it just needs to be pointed at something real. Here is the honest version, which is still hopeful.

Extinct

Gone everywhere, permanently. No garden, seed mix, or habitat plan reverses this. It is the one outcome nothing on this page can undo.

Extirpated

Gone from one region, but still alive elsewhere. This is where habitat work can genuinely matter — well-planned flower and nesting corridors can let a species recolonize ground it lost.

Declining / endangered

Still present, shrinking fast. This is the highest-leverage moment for action — several Bombus species and Hawaiian Hylaeus species are here now.

A number of bee lineages — several Hawaiian Hylaeus (yellow-faced bee) species among them — have not been confirmed in the wild for years and are feared lost, largely to habitat destruction. We name this plainly, without pretending a planting plan changes it, because looking away from real loss isn't peace-aligned — it's just avoidance.

What actually helps: protecting the specific host plants that specialist genera depend on, keeping habitat corridors intact so extirpated-but-extant species can return, and feeding real observations into community science so declines are caught early enough to act on. The Source Trail below links to Bumble Bee Watch, iNaturalist, and the IUCN SSC Wild Bee Specialist Group — all real, all free, all more rigorous than any single tool like this one.

Step-by-Step Build 🛠️

1. Pick your bee families and genera

Use the Genus Directory to shortlist genera plausible for your region and season, not just your continent-scale family list.

2. Build three bloom windows

Spring must feed mining and mason bees. Summer must feed bumble, leafcutter, carpenter, and sweat bees. Fall must feed queen bumble bees and late-season solitary bees.

3. Layer flower shapes

Flat composites and umbels feed short-tongued bees. Tubular flowers feed long-tongued bees. Complex legumes recruit bumble and leafcutter bees. Buzz-pollination flowers support Bombus especially well. Oil-flowers (Malpighiaceae) and squash blooms (Cucurbitaceae) exist specifically for the specialist genera in this atlas.

4. Add nesting substrate

Leave small bare soil patches for ground nesters. Keep hollow and pithy stems standing through winter. Add wood/cavity habitat only if it can be cleaned or replaced to reduce parasite buildup.

5. Remove silent failure modes

No bloom-time insecticides. Reduce mowing. Keep night lighting low. Avoid landscape fabric and thick mulch over all exposed ground.

6. Track arrivals

Use the log below. Count genera and families, not just individual bees. More families and genera present means more ecological redundancy.

Risk Logic: Why Some Bees Need Different Flowers ⚠️

Specialists

Some bees depend on one plant genus or family. Generic nectar mixes may not help them unless their host plant is present — see genera tagged "Host-plant specialist" in the directory.

Short-tongued families

Halictidae, Andrenidae, Colletidae, and many Melittidae need shallow-access flowers: asters, umbels, willow, mustard, and other exposed pollen sources.

Long-tongued families

Apidae and Megachilidae can exploit deeper flowers: legumes, mints, penstemons, bee balm, and orchard blossoms.

Bumble bees

Bumble bees need season-long bloom, nesting cover, overwintering refuge, and flowers that reward buzz pollination.

Important: Honey bee abundance does not prove wild bee recovery. Dense managed hives can sometimes compete with wild bees where floral resources are limited.
